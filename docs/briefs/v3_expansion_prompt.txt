# 2. THE BIGGEST PROBLEM: THERE IS NO ACTUAL WORLD

Currently, almost the entire visual game takes place in Whisperwood Clearing.

**Whisperwood Clearing must be ONE location, not the entire playable universe.**

Add a connected, physically represented woodland world.

## 2.1 Required locations

Implement at least these distinct explorable locations:

| Location               | Core gameplay                                                  |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Whisperwood Clearing   | Player-owned trading post, visitors, commerce, construction    |
| Mama's Den             | Family life, food, sleeping, discipline, hiding possessions    |
| Pebble's Favorite Spot | Sibling time, secrets, shared memories, collecting             |
| Riverside              | Drinking, fishing, stones, water-related activities            |
| Deep Woodland          | Foraging, sticks, exploration, hidden treasures                |
| Cub Playground         | Wrestling, chasing, games, friendships, rivalries              |
| Rival Trading Spot     | Independent traders, competitor stalls, rival deals            |
| Human Trail            | Extremely rare human artifacts, discoveries, parental warnings |

These must be real scenes with distinct maps, objects, characters, interactables, and environmental identities.

**Do not implement locations as text-only popups that say "You visited the den."**

Mama's Den must have an actual visible interior.

A valid den scene might include:

- A sleeping nest.
- Mama's resting area.
- A family food pile.
- Sleeping cubs.
- Hidden storage places.
- Entrances and exits.
- Rocks, roots, bedding, and decorations.
- Family members moving and interacting.
- Objects that the player can inspect and use.

A player must be able to enter Mama's Den, see Mama and family members there, interact with objects, walk around, and leave.

## 2.2 Real travel and navigation

Implement a proper **world map** showing how locations connect.

The player should be able to travel using clearly visible paths, exits, or map controls.

Travel must:

- Change the actual rendered scene.
- Update the player's physical location.
- Advance time according to route distance.
- Use appropriate energy.
- Respect time-of-day accessibility.
- Allow other characters to travel independently.
- Preserve NPC locations and possessions.
- Trigger occasional contextually appropriate encounters.

Prefer intuitive click-to-move controls, with optional keyboard movement where feasible.

Use real destination transitions, not teleportation disguised as a menu.

Travel time should be grounded in the map's relative distances, without making short trips tedious.

## 2.3 Persistent character locations

Cubs must have a current location, destination, schedule, and intention.

A cub who leaves the marketplace should be physically absent from it.

If Fern visits the river, I should not simultaneously see Fern at the trading post.

If Pebble goes home, I should be able to visit him or discover that he is sleeping.

Off-screen characters continue living and making decisions, but preserve consistent world-state rules.

Avoid NPC duplication, unrealistic teleportation, and characters being in two locations simultaneously.

# 3. MAKE EVERY BUTTON PRODUCE A REAL GAMEPLAY RESPONSE

Audit every visible button and player action.

Each action must cause a demonstrable response, such as:

- A character animation.
- A world-state change.
- A location change.
- An inventory transaction.
- Dialogue or a speech bubble.
- A change to needs or relationships.
- A persistent memory.

- A visible construction change.

Not every button must always succeed, but **a refusal must be a valid simulation outcome rather than a fake or broken interaction**.

For example:

"Wrestle Pebble" should start an observable interaction between two cubs, with variable outcomes.

"Hide item" should place the chosen object in a persistent storage location.

"Build bench" should consume resources and create a visible bench.

# 4. NPCS MUST ACQUIRE, OWN, AND PROTECT RARE ITEMS

Currently, I want other cubs to become much more interesting competitors.

They must not exist solely to supply the player with goods.

**NPCs should sometimes find exceptionally rare and valuable items before I do.**

Examples:

- Pebble discovers a legendary polished stone.
- Fern inherits an emotionally precious toy.
- Moss acquires an epic human artifact.
- Sprout finds something extraordinarily rare without understanding its market value.
- Juniper crafts an unusually valuable object.

NPCs must have legitimate ways to acquire rare goods through exploration, crafting, gifts, trading, family events, and discoveries.

They can:

- Treasure them.
- Display them.
- Hide them.
- Refuse to sell them.
- Trade them to another NPC.
- Lose them.
- Gift them.
- Become emotionally attached.
- Become suspicious when others ask about them.

## 4.1 Envy, pride, and jealousy

Add an actual social-emotion system.

The player can feel envious of another cub's possession, and the game should communicate that through optional internal thoughts, dialogue, comparisons, and reactions.

NPCs can envy the player's possessions too.

Envy should influence behavior without automatically overriding autonomy.

Potential reactions include:

- Trying to negotiate.
- Trying to impress another cub.
- Bragging about one's own possessions.
- Becoming jealous of a sibling's success.
- Forming a collecting rivalry.
- Saving resources for a desired item.
- Asking Mama about an object.
- Gossiping about another trader's treasure.

Some items should become socially prestigious without having a fixed monetary price.

# 5. DECEPTION, SECRECY, PERSUASION, AND CONFLICT

Cubs should not always reveal what they own.

Implement distinct inventory states:

- Carried.
- Displayed.
- Stored.
- Hidden.
- Lent out.
- Reserved.
- Known to specific characters.
- Rumored but unverified.

Characters must distinguish between what they actually know and what they merely suspect.

A cub might say:

"I don't have that blue stone."

But actually have it hidden under a tree root.

This should be a real deception mechanic linked to inventories, memories, visibility, and knowledge.

## 5.1 Player social actions

Implement choices such as:

- Ask politely.
- Persuade.
- Negotiate.
- Offer a favor.
- Beg.
- Intimidate.
- Threaten
- Accuse of lying.
- Demand an explanation.
- Confront with evidence.
- Blackmail using a discovered secret.
- Apologize.
- Forgive.
- Make peace.
- Report behavior to Mama.

NPCs can use comparable actions against the player and each other.

Blackmail must require a real known secret, not merely a button that generates free goods.

## 5.2 Autonomous responses

NPCs should be able to:

- Agree.
- Refuse.
- Bargain.
- Lie.
- Call the player's bluff.
- Run away.
- Cry.
- Become angry.
- Retaliate.
- Fight back.
- Seek help.
- Snitch to Mama.
- Tell another cub.
- Form a grudge.
- Hide items more carefully.
- Reconcile later.

Responses must depend on personality, age, courage, relationships, memories, actual knowledge, and the situation.

Physical confrontation should have age-appropriate, non-graphic consequences.

Threatening weaker cubs should not be a universally optimal business strategy. It may produce immediate compliance but lasting distrust, retaliation, or parental intervention.

# 7. MAMA, HUNGER, NEGLECT, AND HARSH CONSEQUENCES

Keep physical needs meaningful.

I specifically want a harsh consequence system where hunger can become genuinely dangerous.

When hunger reaches zero, it should trigger an urgent survival crisis, not just a meaningless red bar.

Mama should have opportunities to notice hunger through observation, schedules, proximity, and prior knowledge.

She may:

- Call the cub home.
- Offer food.
- Force a feeding intervention within the fictional game's rules.
- Restrict exploration.
- Close the trading post temporarily.
- Become angry or worried.
- Refuse help in extreme relationship or neglect scenarios.

If Mama does not intervene and the cub remains without food, starvation and death can eventually occur.

However:

- Zero hunger must not produce unexplained instant death.
- Health deterioration should be visible and logically timed.
- Outcomes should depend on the actual situation, not only a single arbitrary trust score.
- The player should receive meaningful warnings and opportunities to respond.
- Death must have an appropriate game-over, restart, or reload flow.
- Mama's behavior and history must remain internally consistent.
- Avoid unavoidable starvation loops.

Add a configurable consequence-severity option if useful, with the requested harsh simulation available.

# 19. FINAL DESIGN STANDARD

**The Little Trading Post is not just a trading game with bear pictures.**

It is a miniature woodland society inhabited by baby bears.

The player should be able to wake up in Mama's Den, argue with a littermate, eat breakfast, discover that another cub obtained an extraordinarily rare treasure, travel through the woods, visit a rival, attempt a complicated negotiation, hide a suspicious object from Mama, return home, and discover that the other cubs have continued living without them.

NPCs must have their own possessions, secrets, ambitions, family connections, memories, and changing relationships.

The player must be able to own, manage, expand, and customize the trading post while participating in the larger community.

The world should be cute, readable, reactive, occasionally unfair, and full of meaningful surprises.

**Build an actual explorable game. Make the systems interact. Audit what already exists. Research before redesigning. Test actual behavior. Deliver the playable result, not just an impressive description of what it might become.**

Begin immediately with source inspection, an honest audit, and the highest-impact implementation work. Do not repeatedly ask for approval.
